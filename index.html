Restoration Druid on the WoW Forever beta

What 186 community members said about how the spec feels, with the statistics behind every claim.

Poll run in the Forever Restoration Druid channel of the Classic Druid Discord. Beta build 1.60.1.70170. A community survey of the Restoration Druid beta.

41 Love it104 Like it38 Mixed3 Dislike it

Each dot is one respondent, coloured by their overall rating of the spec on the current build.

Reception is clearly positive: 78% of respondents love or like the current design (95% CI 71–83%), and only 3 of 186 dislike it. The satisfaction that exists is conditional, though. Concerns concentrate on two things: what Restoration Druid brings to a raid that other healers and druid specs do not, and the state of its talent trees.

This is a self-selected sample from one enthusiast community (186 responses, 84% min-maxers or experienced players). It describes how engaged Restoration Druid players feel, not the wider player base. Every figure carries its sample size and uncertainty.

Strong evidence

Each issue is graded by its evidence: strong is a clear measured effect that survives correction, moderate a clear pattern with a caveat, tentative an idea from a small number of unprompted comments.

Why bring a Restoration Druid?

The most common uncertainty, and the one players tie to raid slots.

Players do not ask for more healing. They ask for a reason to be in the raid.

The most common uncertainty in the poll is the role question. 47% are unsure of the reasons to bring a Restoration Druid over other druid specs, and the worry is concrete: those who are unsure about the role are far more likely to worry about securing a raid slot (40% against 8%). Slot worry also goes with a lower rating of the spec (56% positive among the worried, against 85% of the rest).

Reading the pattern. The raid-buff request is probably the same question asked another way. Other druid specs already bring raid-wide buffs, such as Moonkin Aura and Leader of the Pack, and Faerie Fire, so Restoration Druid has no buff of its own that another druid does not also provide. Several commenters make this comparison directly. The poll shows the request and the role uncertainty together; it does not test that one causes the other.

What respondents said

  • Other healers bring raid-wide buffs, cooldowns or damage contribution, and Innervate and combat resurrection alone do not justify a slot over a Paladin, Shaman or Priest.
  • A Feral or Balance druid already supplies the same druid utility, so a dedicated Restoration slot is hard to justify.

Early talent rows

Described as disappointing, worthless or taken only as prerequisites.

The first talent rows feel like something to get through, not something to choose.

The poll did not ask about talent rows directly, so this comes from free text. 5 of 80 commenters (6%) criticise the early rows, and both coders agreed on every one. They call the first two rows disappointing or worthless and say some talents are taken only as prerequisites for later ones.

  • The first talents should be ones players want to take, not ones they must take to reach later talents.
  • Newer players are hit hardest: the early game offers little reason to put points into Restoration, and levelling as a healer feels inaccessible when early points are costly.

Tentative evidence Unprompted comments, so the count is a floor, not a measure.

Build variety and mandatory talents

Too many picks feel forced, and the trees feel cookie-cutter.

Build choice feels thin, with too many talents that are mandatory in practice.

4 comments (two coders agreeing) describe a lack of build variety or picks that are forced: a talent link added from Improved Rejuvenation to Improved Regrowth that forces a pick to play the Regrowth build, early talents taken only as prerequisites, not enough points to take the damage and mana talents some players want, and a tree described as cookie-cutter with little reason to take certain talents at all.

The structured answers point the same way without settling it. Build and talent options are the second most requested area for more focus (30%, 23–37%), a 12-point increase over the 18% who historically enjoy it. That gap does not clear correction for multiple tests (Holm p = .050), so read it as a direction.

Talents players will not use

Both Tranquility talents are expected to be skipped by a majority.

Of the sixteen talents polled, six are expected to go unused by a large share of players.

The poll listed sixteen talents (the trees hold many more, treated as taken in all scenarios and not asked about) and asked which respondents do not expect to use. The answers concentrate on six: Subtlety (67%), Improved Tranquility (65%), Tranquil Spirit (59%), Insect Swarm (53%), Moonglow (39%) and Improved Wrath (37%). The other ten were each named by 10% or fewer, and Reflection by no one. Among those who answered, 69% (95% CI 61–75%) expect to skip three or more of the six, and 40% expect to skip both Tranquility talents.

The clearest signal is the two Tranquility talents: 65% expect to skip Improved Tranquility and 59% Tranquil Spirit, and Tranquility is the most common free-text theme (18 comments). Insect Swarm, Moonglow, Improved Wrath and Subtlety improve damage or threat, so a healer skipping them may reflect role fit as well as dislike.

About the poll

Who answered, and how far the results go.

186 members of the Forever Restoration Druid channel of the Classic Druid Discord answered an anonymous poll about the current beta build. It is a self-selected sample (84% min-maxers or experienced players), so it describes how engaged Restoration Druid players feel, not the wider player base. Comments are paraphrased, not quoted.

The technical report holds every statistic behind the findings: methods, interval estimates, standard deviations, tests with multiple-comparison correction, the regression model, the free-text coding and the limits of the data. The plain-language version is in the Findings tab.

Method

186 responses from one Discord channel, analysed with methods suited to small samples of ordinal answers.

The poll was an anonymous web form with 18 questions: about the player, the current beta build, playstyle, raiding, and an optional free-text box. It was posted only in the Forever Restoration Druid channel of the Classic Druid Discord, so it is a convenience sample. The intervals reported here describe sampling noise within this self-selected group, not uncertainty about all Restoration Druid players. Only the overall rating was compulsory, so each result uses the respondents who answered that item and the denominator is shown with it. Question wording is in the technical appendix.

  • Proportions carry Wilson 95% intervals; means carry 10,000-resample bootstrap intervals and standard deviations.
  • Group comparisons and associations use rank-based and chi-square tests with permutation p-values, because the answers are ordinal and several groups are small.
  • Multiplicity. Tests were run in pre-defined families and Holm-adjusted; headline claims rely on the adjusted values.
  • Multivariable model. An ordinal logistic regression with five pre-specified predictors and bootstrap intervals.
  • Free text was coded against an 18-code codebook in two independent passes, with agreement measured by Cohen's kappa.

Significance level is 0.05, two-sided. Tests that are exploratory are labelled as such.

Reading the numbers

95% confidence interval (CI)The range of values consistent with the data. A wide interval means a small or noisy sample. Proportions use Wilson intervals; means use a 10,000-resample bootstrap.
Standard deviation (SD)How spread out individual answers are around the mean. SD describes people; it is not the uncertainty of the mean (that is the CI).
Effect sizeHow big a difference is, independent of sample size. Reported beside every test: rank-biserial r (0.1 small, 0.3 medium, 0.5 large), epsilon squared (0.01, 0.06, 0.14), Cramér's V and odds ratios.
Adjusted p-valueWith many tests, some will look significant by chance. Holm adjusts for the whole family of related tests; Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) controls the false discovery rate. Headline claims rely on Holm.
Kendall's WAgreement among respondents' rankings, from 0 (none) to 1 (everyone ranks identically).
Cohen's kappa (κ)Agreement between two coders beyond chance, from 0 to 1. Above 0.8 is conventionally read as very good.

Who responded

An experienced, raid-focused group, with few players from the smaller content types.

The sample is experienced and raid-focused. 84% describe themselves as min-maxers or experienced, 88% mostly play PvE, and 85% had logged in to the current build. The 28 who had not played the build answered from the published design alone, so their ratings are less informed; the main results are shown with and without them.

Overall sentiment

78% love or like the spec; the ratings barely vary with who is answering.

78% of respondents love or like the spec, and the rating distribution is tight: the mean is 3.98 (SD 0.70) and the median, mode and both quartiles all sit at “Like it”. Because 56% chose the same option, the interquartile range is zero, so the mean and SD are the more informative summaries here, with the share of "Love it" (22%) showing how much enthusiasm sits above the plateau.

Ratings sit well above the neutral midpoint: Wilcoxon signed-rank against 3.0 gives rank-biserial r = 0.97, p <.001 (n = 148 non-neutral respondents), and an exact binomial test that more than half are positive gives p <.001. With a ceiling this tight these tests are almost uninformative on their own; the interval on the share positive is the useful result.

Ratings barely vary with who is answering. Experience level showed no trend (Spearman ρ = −0.07, 95% CI [−0.22, 0.08]) and content type did not differ (p = .416), though PvP and open-world players are too few to say anything firm. Players who had not yet logged in rated the spec slightly lower (mean 3.75 against 4.03; Holm-adjusted .281), but the share positive is similar (78% of players against 75% of non-players), so the headline result does not depend on them.

What goes with satisfaction

How respondents judge their toolkit tracks their rating more than anything else.

The clearest pattern is the link between how respondents judge their toolkit and how they rate the spec. Among the 115 who say they have been given adequate healing and utility tools, 91% love or like the spec (mean 4.23). Among the 16 who say they have not, 25% do (mean 3.25). The 53 who say there is not enough information sit in between (mean 3.70).

Statistical detail

Toolkit judgement vs rating: Kruskal-Wallis ε² = 0.222 (large), permutation p <.001, Holm-adjusted p = .001. This is the largest association with the rating and survives Holm correction across the family of twelve tests.

Raid-slot worry: 56% positive among the 43 who are worried against 85% among the 104 who are not (ε² = 0.075, raw p <.001, Holm .011, BH .006). It also survives Holm correction, and remains after holding other answers constant (below).

Baseline UI: ratings fall slightly as the UI is judged less adequate (Spearman ρ = −0.20, 95% CI [−0.34, −0.04], raw p = .008, Holm .080). Treat as tentative: it does not survive Holm correction.

Holding the other answers constant

An ordinal logistic model puts five answers in at once so that overlapping effects are separated. Judging the toolkit inadequate or being unsure about it is associated with about one fifth of the odds of a higher rating (odds ratio 0.16, 95% bootstrap CI 0.06–0.29), and so is worrying about a raid slot, at roughly one quarter of the odds (odds ratio 0.28, 0.10–0.62). Finding the baseline UI unplayable without addons is also associated with lower ratings (odds ratio 0.43, 95% bootstrap CI 0.17–0.88), although its bivariate test did not survive correction, so read it as tentative. Not having played the build points the same way but is imprecise: its interval includes 1. Experience level adds nothing. The model as a whole is clearly better than none (likelihood ratio χ²(5) = 55.0, p <.001; McFadden pseudo-R² = 0.16).

Read this model with care. It is exploratory. There are 39 respondents in the lowest rating category for five predictors, below the usual ten events per predictor, so estimates are imprecise and may be inflated. No non-player chose "Love it" (0 of 28), which makes the not-played estimate partly an artefact of that empty cell. The data are cross-sectional, so these are associations, not causes: low satisfaction may colour how people judge the toolkit just as a weak toolkit may lower satisfaction.

Playstyle rankings

Sustained rot-fight healing is the most enjoyed playstyle, Healing Touch the least, and Lifebloom is the most divisive.

Respondents ranked six historical Restoration Druid playstyles from most to least enjoyed. Consistently healing through sustained raid damage (the rot-fight profile, with Sapphiron as the example) was ranked first by 38% and in the top three by 83%. Healing Touch single-target healing was ranked last by 55% and in the top three by only 11%.

Lifebloom and HoT upkeep is a divider. Its mean rank (3.02) is middling, but its SD (1.80) is the largest of the six: 30% rank it first and 14% rank it last. A mean would hide that.

Statistical detail

The six playstyles are not ranked equally: Friedman χ²(5) = 256.7, p <.001, with Kendall's W = 0.28 (95% bootstrap CI 0.23–0.33), a moderate level of agreement. Rot-fight healing is ranked above every other playstyle and Healing Touch below every other (all Holm-adjusted p < .001). The middle of the table is less certain: Lifebloom, proactive blanketing and Regrowth cannot be told apart statistically (Holm-adjusted p between .06 and .44 for the 2 pairs that are not significant). The full 15-pair table is in the appendix.

Priorities and strengths

Players want to keep the feel and mobility, and they ask for more raid utility, cooldowns and build choice.

Two separate questions asked what players historically enjoy about the spec and what they want more focus on before launch. Read together they separate what players value from what they ask for more of. General feel (68% enjoy it), mobility (57%), raid healing (52%) and unique spec identity (52%) are the most-enjoyed strengths, and few ask for more of them. The requests are different: hybrid gameplay (32%), build and talent options (30%), unique spec identity (27%), cooldown options (25%) and raid buffs (25%).

Raid buffs stand out. Only 5% historically enjoy raid buffs as part of the spec (the spec has not had them), yet 25% want more focus on them, a 19-point difference (95% CI 12 to 27; Holm p <.001). It is a new request, not a defence of something old. Cooldown options show the same pattern (25% want against 9% enjoy; Holm p = .001), and build options lean the same way (30% against 18%) but not significantly after correction (Holm p = .050).

The requests do not track dissatisfaction: none of the eleven focus areas is associated with the overall rating (all BH-adjusted p ≥ .31), so they come as much from satisfied respondents as from mixed ones. They read as improvement asks, not complaints.

What people are unsure about

Nearly half do not see why a raid would bring a Restoration Druid over another spec.

Respondents were asked what, if anything, they are unsure about. 47% are unsure of the reasons to bring a Restoration Druid over other druid specs, making it the most common uncertainty, and 45% are unsure about downranking. Only 16% say everything is clear. Unclear tooltips (6%), mana management (13%) and how gear works (5%) are minor by comparison.

The role uncertainty goes with raid-slot worry. Among those unsure why to bring a Restoration Druid, 40% also worry about a raid slot; among everyone else only 8% do (odds ratio 7.7, 95% CI 3.2–18.9; Fisher's exact p <.001). This is the strongest association in the survey, and it ties the role question to a concrete worry, not an abstract one.

Talents players expect to skip

A small cluster of talents attracts almost all of the skips, including both Tranquility talents.

The poll listed sixteen talents out of the many in the trees; the rest were treated as taken in all scenarios and not asked about, so these rates cover only those sixteen. The skip pattern is sharply concentrated. Six talents account for almost all of it: Subtlety (67%), Improved Tranquility (65%), Tranquil Spirit (59%), Insect Swarm (53%), Moonglow (39%) and Improved Wrath (37%). Ten of the sixteen polled talents were named by 9% or fewer, and Reflection by no one.

Not every skip signals discontent. Insect Swarm, Moonglow and Improved Wrath improve damaging spells and Subtlety reduces threat, so a healer skipping them may reflect role fit. The two Tranquility talents (Improved Tranquility and Tranquil Spirit) are healing talents that a majority expect to skip, which is a clearer signal, and it matches the Tranquility concerns in the comments and in the raiding questions below.

Raiding questions

Fewer than one in ten can see themselves pressing Tranquility in a raid.

On the question of a raid slot, 23% are worried (18–30%), 21% are unsure and 56% are not, so just under half are either worried or unsure. Willingness to off-spec when called upon is high (58% yes), and 52% enjoy DPSing in low-healing periods.

Tranquility

Only 10 respondents (5.5%; 95% CI 3.0–9.8%) can see themselves pressing Tranquility in raids as it is implemented. Most picture using it in five-man content (33%) or very occasionally (37%), and 25% would never press it.

Baseline UI

Only 21% find the baseline UI fine for tracking HoTs and procs. 54% need basic tweaks and 25% (19–32%) call it unplayable without external addons. This did not differ between players and non-players of the build or between experience levels.

Free-text comments

80 comments, coded twice, led by Tranquility and raid utility.

80 respondents (43%) left a usable free-text comment. Comments were coded against an 18-code codebook by two independent passes, with high agreement (pooled κ = 0.93; both coders assigned exactly the same set of codes to 81% of comments).

Respondents were told comments would only be seen by the organiser, so this section paraphrases and does not quote.

What respondents said

Raid utility and raid slots (15 comments). Respondents say other healers bring raid-wide buffs, cooldowns or damage contribution, and that Innervate and combat resurrection alone do not justify a slot over a Paladin, Shaman or Priest, or over a feral or balance druid who brings similar utility. Requests range from a raid-wide buff to an external or damage-mitigation cooldown or offensive utility. Several worry about a tight slot count in smaller raids, and some expect Restoration Druid to be treated as a temporary progression healer, with Balance Druids swapping in when more healing is needed.

Tranquility (18 comments). Concerns are its group-only scope, range and long cooldown, overlap with Wild Growth and the capstone talent, and little reason to press it in a raid. Suggestions include a raid-wide long-cooldown version, a range talent, or making it a useful button for builds without Wild Growth.

Positive overall (8 comments). Respondents describe it as the best iteration of the spec, like the HoT-centred design, say five-man healing feels good, and appreciate baseline HoT crits and Omen of Clarity.

Tree of Life form (7 comments). Most want a cosmetic, optional form (or a way to keep the old look) and do not want tree form to be mandatory. A minority want a new mechanic attached to it, such as a stationary channelled aura.

Hybrid, off-spec and PvP (8 comments). The hybrid tax discourages off-spec play, hybridising feels poor in PvP (though one values the Vanilla balance/resto hybrid as competitive in 1v1), and HoT-based PvP healing needs dispel protection. Others raise catweaving, a restokin flex-healer idea, and earlier dual spec.

Toolkit, HoTs and downranking (11 comments). Opinions are mixed: one asks to remove downranking, others want Healing Touch and Regrowth to stay relevant, find GCD-locked blanketing unfun, or miss dynamic and snapshotting HoT play. One asks for a mana-efficient way to focus heals on the tank to replace rank-4 Healing Touch, and one does not want a Nourish-led ramping playstyle.

Talent tree design (11 comments). The first two rows feel like mandatory fillers rather than choices players want, build diversity feels limited, specific talents (Moonglow, Genesis against Improved Wrath, the Healing Touch talents) are called unattractive, and Tranquil Spirit reduces cost on rarely used spells.

Lifebloom and Efflorescence. Four comments want Lifebloom back, ideally limited to a single target, and three oppose it (one because it feels worst to maintain, one because they would not play with it on tanks, one wanting neither Lifebloom nor tree form). Four want Efflorescence, including as an alternative to Tranquility.

Mana and downtime (4 comments). Respondents say the spec has no good downtime option in dungeons compared with Priests (wanding), Shamans and Paladins, and that reduced-cost Wrath is not a real equivalent to wanding. One adds that, with no downranking to fish for Clearcasting, meleeing is the best current downtime option.

Levelling (2 comments). Two respondents worry about levelling as a healer: dungeon experience reductions make it costly to spend early talent points on Restoration talents.

Spec diversity and racials (3 comments). One expects a single dominant raid spec and weak racials; another flags a racial that may multiply the mana from Innervate and asks for clarification before launch; a third worries that a mana-regeneration racial could be overpowered in mana-limited encounters, and that hunter pets gaining Faerie Fire reduce the need to bring a druid.

Interpretation

Who comments? Commenters and non-commenters rated the spec the same (mean 3.98 against 3.99), but commenters skew towards min-maxers (56% against 25%). Comment frequency is therefore a weak guide to how widespread a view is; use it for the range of ideas, not their prevalence.

Do comments agree with the structured answers? Raid utility is the second most common comment theme (15 of 80) and also the largest structured signal (role uncertainty 47%, raid-slot worry 23%, raid-buff focus 25%). Tranquility is the most common theme (18) and matches the raid-use and talent-skip results.

Limits of the data

What this poll can and cannot support.

Representativeness

The poll was posted in a single Discord channel for Restoration Druid enthusiasts. That makes it a convenience sample with unknown coverage: the channel's size is unknown, so a response rate cannot be computed, and people who chose to answer are probably more engaged than those who did not. Nothing was weighted. The largest 95% margin of error for a proportion at n = 186 would be about ±7 points under random sampling, but because this is not a random sample, treat that as a lower bound on the true uncertainty.

What this sample can and cannot detect

With 186 respondents and a two-sided test at 0.05, a comparison of two equal groups reaches 80% power only for a medium effect (Cohen's d about 0.41); for the 28 non-players against the rest it is 0.74, and for the 10 newest players it is 1.0. Many "no difference" findings therefore mean "no large difference detected", not "no difference".

What this poll cannot show

  • How the wider player base feels. The sample is a single engaged channel with no response rate.
  • Whether changes would raise satisfaction. All links are associations at one point in time, so a weak toolkit may lower satisfaction or low satisfaction may colour how the toolkit is judged.
  • How common the minority themes are. Comment authors skew towards min-maxers.
  • Differences between small groups (new players, PvP and open-world players).
  • Opinion on talents outside the sixteen polled. The trees hold many more talents; the rest were treated as taken in all scenarios and were not asked about, so the skip rates describe only the sixteen listed.

Other cautions

  • The five-point rating has a ceiling: nobody chose "Hate it" and 56% chose "Like it", so group differences are hard to detect and standard deviations are small.
  • "Want more focus" and "historically enjoy" use the same eleven options but ask different questions, so they are compared as priorities against strengths only.
  • Free-text coding was done by two language-model coders working independently from the same codebook. High agreement shows the codebook is applied consistently; it does not replace a human check.
